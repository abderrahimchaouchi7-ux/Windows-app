# 1. How should the student use this guide?

Do not memorize formulas alone. For every rule in this guide, ask three questions:

1. **When is it used?**
2. **What conditions must be satisfied?**
3. **How should it be written on an examination paper?**

A sound revision method is:

- Read the rule summary.
- Watch one worked example.
- Close the solution and try the exercise independently.
- Compare your steps with the solution, not only the final answer.
- Record the mistake you made in a personal error notebook.
- Solve a second exercise based on the same idea but with different data.

## Golden rule

> An exercise is not truly solved because you understood a video; it is solved when you can write the solution again without watching the video.

# 3. Mathematical language and basic notation

## 3.1 Intervals

- Open interval:

\[
]a,b[=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\}
\]

- Closed interval:

\[
[a,b]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\le x\le b\}
\]

- Half-open intervals:

\[
]a,b]\quad\text{or}\quad[a,b[
\]

- Infinity is not an ordinary real number. Therefore we write:

\[
-\infty\quad\text{and}\quad+\infty
\]

and do not treat expressions such as \(\infty+1\) as ordinary arithmetic.

## 3.2 Useful logical symbols

- \(P\Rightarrow Q\): if \(P\) is true, then \(Q\) is true.
- \(P\Longleftrightarrow Q\): \(P\) is equivalent to \(Q\), meaning both directions hold.
- \(\forall\): for every.
- \(\exists\): there exists.
- \(\in\): belongs to.
- \(\subset\): is a subset of.

## 3.3 Statements that must be distinguished

- “A point belongs to a curve” means its coordinates satisfy the equation of the curve.
- “Points are collinear” means that they lie on the same straight line.
- “Two vectors are parallel” means one is a scalar multiple of the other.
- “Two lines are perpendicular” means their dot product is zero when represented by direction vectors.
- “A function is increasing” does not mean that its values are positive; it means that its values increase as the variable moves to the right.

# 4. General methodology for solving any exercise

## Stage 1: Read the question

Read the entire exercise once without calculating. Underline:

- Numerical data.
- Conditions.
- Names of functions, points, and sequences.
- The verbs: calculate, prove, deduce, study, discuss, determine, represent, solve.

## Stage 2: Identify the topic

Ask yourself:

- Is this a function problem?
- Does it require a derivative?
- Is it a probability problem?
- Are points and vectors involved?
- Is it a sequence?
- Is it a limit?

Identifying the topic prevents the use of a rule from another chapter.

## Stage 3: Make a short plan

Before writing, formulate a plan mentally, for example:

> I will determine the domain, calculate the derivative, study its sign, and then construct the variation table.

Or:

> I will calculate two vectors, check whether they are proportional, and then conclude collinearity.

## Stage 4: Write the solution

Write each equality on a separate line and state the reason for an important transition:

- By definition.
- By the property.
- By the theorem.
- Since ...
- Therefore ...
- Consequently ...

## Stage 5: Answer the question explicitly

End with a complete sentence, for example:

> Therefore, the function is decreasing on \(]-\infty,1]\) and increasing on \([1,+\infty[\).

Do not force the marker to infer your conclusion from a calculation.

# 5. How to write a mathematical proof

## 5.1 Direct proof

The structure is:

1. Start from the given information.
2. Apply a definition, property, or theorem.
3. Reach the required statement.
4. Write a conclusion.

### Useful wording

> We know that ...
>
> By ... we obtain ...
>
> Hence ...
>
> Therefore, it has been proved that ...

## 5.2 Proof by contradiction

To prove a statement \(P\):

1. Assume the opposite of \(P\).
2. Deduce consequences from that assumption.
3. Reach a contradiction with a given fact or a known result.
4. Reject the assumption.
5. Conclude that the original statement is true.

### Model wording

> Assume the opposite of what is required. This gives ... , which contradicts the given fact ... . Therefore the assumption is false, and the required statement is true.

## 5.3 Proof by contrapositive

To prove:

\[
P\Rightarrow Q
\]

it can sometimes be easier to prove:

\[
\overline{Q}\Rightarrow\overline{P}
\]

Use this only when the logical transformation is correct and suitable for the lesson.

## 5.4 Counterexample

To disprove a universal statement, one valid counterexample is enough.

For example, the statement “every increasing function is positive” is false: \(f(x)=x\) is increasing on \(\mathbb{R}\), but it is negative when \(x<0\).

## 5.5 Equivalence proof

If you must prove:

\[
P\Longleftrightarrow Q
\]

write two separate directions:

- First: assume \(P\) and prove \(Q\).
- Second: assume \(Q\) and prove \(P\).

## 5.6 Analytical and geometric proof

- **Analytical proof:** uses coordinates, equations, and algebra.
- **Vector proof:** uses vectors, proportionality, and the dot product.
- **Geometric proof:** uses properties of shapes, angles, parallelism, and perpendicularity.

Choosing the shortest clear method is part of solving the problem.

## 5.7 What is not a proof

The following, by themselves, are not enough:

- “Obviously.”
- “The drawing shows it,” when an analytical proof is required.
- “By experiment,” to prove a universal statement.
- Writing only the final result.
- Using one example to prove a general rule.

# 6. Essential algebra toolkit

## 6.1 Standard identities

\[
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2
\]

\[
(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2
\]

\[
(a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2
\]

\[
a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)
\]

\[
a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)
\]

## 6.2 Factoring

The main methods are:

1. Factor out a common factor.
2. Use the difference of two squares.
3. Use a standard identity.
4. Group terms.
5. Find a root of a polynomial.

### Example

\[
x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)
\]

## 6.3 Sign of a product

To study the sign of:

\[
(x-a)(x-b)
\]

mark the roots \(a,b\) on a number line and use:

- A product is positive when the factors have the same sign.
- A product is negative when the factors have opposite signs.

## 6.4 Zero-product rule

If:

\[
AB=0
\]

then:

\[
A=0\quad\text{or}\quad B=0
\]

## 6.5 Algebraic fractions

Before cancelling a factor, state the restriction that makes the denominator nonzero.

For example:

\[
\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}
\]

with:

\[
x\ne2
\]

Then:

\[
\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2
\]

while keeping the condition \(x\ne2\).

# 7. Functions and graphs

## 7.1 Definition of a function

A function \(f\) associates exactly one number \(f(x)\) with every \(x\) in its domain.

We write:

\[
f:D_f\to\mathbb{R}
\]

and its graph is:

\[
C_f=\{M(x,f(x)):x\in D_f\}
\]

## 7.2 Operations on functions

For functions \(f\) and \(g\) on a suitable domain:

\[
(f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)
\]

\[
(f-g)(x)=f(x)-g(x)
\]

\[
(fg)(x)=f(x)g(x)
\]

\[
\left(\frac fg\right)(x)=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)},\qquad g(x)\ne0
\]

\[
(\lambda f)(x)=\lambda f(x)
\]

## 7.3 Domains of operations

- The domain of \(f+g\) is the intersection of the domains of \(f\) and \(g\).
- The domain of \(fg\) is the same intersection.
- The domain of \(f/g\) is the intersection with all values satisfying \(g(x)=0\) removed.

## 7.4 Reference functions

You should know the general shape and variation of:

- \(f(x)=c\): constant function.
- \(f(x)=ax+b\): affine function.
- \(f(x)=x^2\): decreasing on \(]-\infty,0]\) and increasing on \([0,+\infty[\).
- \(f(x)=x^3\): increasing on \(\mathbb{R}\).
- \(f(x)=\frac1x\): defined on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\).
- \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\): defined on \([0,+\infty[\).
- \(f(x)=\sin x\) and \(f(x)=\cos x\): periodic functions.

## 7.5 Graph transformations

If the graph \(C_f\) is known:

- \(f(x)+k\): vertical translation upward when \(k>0\).
- \(f(x)-k\): vertical translation downward when \(k>0\).
- \(f(x-a)\): horizontal translation to the right by \(a\).
- \(f(x+a)\): horizontal translation to the left by \(a\).
- \(-f(x)\): reflection in the x-axis.
- \(f(-x)\): reflection in the y-axis.

## 7.6 Symmetry

- The function is even if:

\[
f(-x)=f(x)
\]

Its graph is symmetric about the y-axis.

- The function is odd if:

\[
f(-x)=-f(x)
\]

Its graph is centrally symmetric about the origin.

- If the graph is centrally symmetric about \(I(a,b)\), use the appropriate central-symmetry condition after translating coordinates to the point \(I\).

## 7.7 Solving an equation graphically

To solve:

\[
f(x)=g(x)
\]

find the x-coordinates of the intersection points of \(C_f\) and \(C_g\).

To solve:

\[
f(x)>g(x)
\]

identify the intervals on which the graph of \(f\) lies above the graph of \(g\).

## 7.8 Full function-study method

When asked to study a function, use the requested parts in this order:

1. Domain.
2. Limits.
3. Asymptotes.
4. Derivative.
5. Sign of the derivative.
6. Variation table.
7. Special values.
8. Intersections with axes.
9. Graph.

Do not perform unnecessary work when time is limited, but never omit a condition needed for correctness.

# 8. Differentiation and applications

## 8.1 Definition of the derivative

The derivative of \(f\) at \(x_0\) is:

\[
f'(x_0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}
\]

when this finite limit exists.

## 8.2 Geometric interpretation

\(f'(x_0)\) is the slope of the tangent to the graph at:

\[
A(x_0,f(x_0))
\]

## 8.3 Equation of the tangent

\[
T:y=f'(x_0)(x-x_0)+f(x_0)
\]

## 8.4 Derivatives of basic functions

\[
(c)'=0
\]

\[
(x)'=1
\]

\[
(ax+b)'=a
\]

\[
(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}
\]

\[
\left(\frac1x\right)'=-\frac1{x^2}
\]

\[
(\sqrt{x})'=\frac1{2\sqrt{x}},\qquad x>0
\]

\[
(\sin x)'=\cos x
\]

\[
(\cos x)'=-\sin x
\]

## 8.5 Differentiation rules

\[
(f+g)'=f'+g'
\]

\[
(\lambda f)'=\lambda f'
\]

\[
(fg)'=f'g+fg'
\]

\[
\left(\frac fg\right)'=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2},\qquad g\ne0
\]

## 8.6 Derivative and variation

- If \(f'(x)>0\) on an interval, then \(f\) is increasing there.
- If \(f'(x)<0\) on an interval, then \(f\) is decreasing there.
- If the derivative changes from negative to positive, there is a local minimum.
- If it changes from positive to negative, there is a local maximum.

## 8.7 Optimization problems

Steps:

1. Define the variable.
2. Express the quantity to optimize in terms of the variable.
3. Determine the feasible interval.
4. Calculate the derivative.
5. Study its sign.
6. Determine the maximum or minimum.
7. Interpret the answer in the context of the problem.

Do not write only “the extreme value is ...”; explain what it represents in the problem.

# 9. Probability and random variables

## 9.1 Basic concepts

- Random experiment: an experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty.
- Sample space: \(\Omega\).
- Event: a subset of \(\Omega\).
- Impossible event: \(\varnothing\).
- Certain event: \(\Omega\).

## 9.2 Equally likely outcomes

\[
P(A)=\frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{number of possible outcomes}}
\]

## 9.3 Probability properties

\[
0\le P(A)\le1
\]

\[
P(\Omega)=1
\]

\[
P(\varnothing)=0
\]

\[
P(\overline{A})=1-P(A)
\]

For compound events, when this relation is part of the lesson:

\[
P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)
\]

## 9.4 Frequency

The experimental frequency of event \(A\) is:

\[
f(A)=\frac{n_A}{n}
\]

where \(n_A\) is the number of occurrences of \(A\), and \(n\) is the number of trials.

## 9.5 Random variable

If the random variable \(X\) takes values \(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n\) with probabilities \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\), then:

\[
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i=1
\]

## 9.6 Expected value

\[
E(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ip_i
\]

## 9.7 Variance

\[
V(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2
\]

where:

\[
E(X^2)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2p_i
\]

## 9.8 Standard deviation

\[
\sigma(X)=\sqrt{V(X)}
\]

## 9.9 How to write a probability solution

1. Define the events or the random variable.
2. Write a probability table if necessary.
3. Check that the probabilities add up to 1.
4. Write the formula before substitution.
5. Calculate accurately.
6. Interpret the result when the problem is contextual.

# 10. Barycenter in the plane

## 10.1 Barycenter of two weighted points

If:

\[
\alpha+\beta\ne0
\]

then the barycenter of:

\[
(A,\alpha),(B,\beta)
\]

is the point \(G\) satisfying:

\[
\alpha\overrightarrow{GA}+\beta\overrightarrow{GB}=\vec0
\]

## 10.2 Coordinates of the barycenter

If:

\[
A(x_A,y_A),\qquad B(x_B,y_B)
\]

then:

\[
G\left(\frac{\alpha x_A+\beta x_B}{\alpha+\beta},
\frac{\alpha y_A+\beta y_B}{\alpha+\beta}\right)
\]

## 10.3 Barycenter of three points

If:

\[
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\ne0
\]

then:

\[
G\left(\frac{\alpha x_A+\beta x_B+\gamma x_C}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma},
\frac{\alpha y_A+\beta y_B+\gamma y_C}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\right)
\]

## 10.4 Methodology

For “determine the coordinates of the barycenter”:

1. Add the coefficients.
2. Check that their sum is nonzero.
3. Substitute the coordinates in the formula.
4. Simplify each coordinate.
5. Write the point in an organized form.

For “prove collinearity using the barycenter”:

- Express the point as a suitable barycenter.
- Or prove that two vectors are proportional.
- Or use the fact that a barycenter lies on the line carrying the weighted points.

## 10.5 Locus of a point

If the condition contains distances or vectors, let:

\[
M(x,y)
\]

Translate the geometric condition into an equation in \(x,y\). Simplify it and identify the locus.

# 11. Limits and asymptotic behavior

## 11.1 Limit rules

When the limits exist:

\[
\lim(f+g)=\lim f+\lim g
\]

\[
\lim(fg)=(\lim f)(\lim g)
\]

\[
\lim\left(\frac fg\right)=\frac{\lim f}{\lim g}
\]

provided the denominator is nonzero in the limiting process.

## 11.2 Indeterminate forms

Common forms include:

\[
\frac00,\qquad \frac{\infty}{\infty},\qquad \infty-\infty
\]

They do not mean that the limit does not exist; they mean that the expression must be transformed.

## 11.3 Methods for removing indeterminacy

### Factoring and cancelling

\[
\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2
\]

### Multiplying by the conjugate

For expressions such as:

\[
\sqrt{x+1}-\sqrt{x}
\]

multiply by:

\[
\sqrt{x+1}+\sqrt{x}
\]

### Dividing by the highest power

When \(x\to\infty\), divide the numerator and denominator of a rational expression by the highest power present.

## 11.4 Horizontal asymptote

If:

\[
\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=\ell
\]

then:

\[
y=\ell
\]

is a horizontal asymptote at \(+\infty\).

## 11.5 Vertical asymptote

If:

\[
\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=\pm\infty
\]

then:

\[
x=a
\]

is a vertical asymptote.

## 11.6 Oblique asymptote

If there are numbers \(a,b\) such that:

\[
\lim_{x\to\infty}[f(x)-(ax+b)]=0
\]

then:

\[
y=ax+b
\]

is an oblique asymptote.

## 11.7 Limit-question procedure

1. Identify the direction of approach.
2. Substitute initially.
3. If an indeterminate form appears, identify it.
4. Choose a method: factoring, conjugate, highest-power division, or limit theorems.
5. Calculate the limit.
6. Relate it to asymptotic behavior if requested.

Do not use L’Hôpital’s rule unless it has been taught and authorized by the teacher; the usual method at this level relies on algebraic transformations and theorems in the program.

# 12. Directed angles and trigonometry

## 12.1 Directed angles

A directed angle between two vectors takes direction into account. It is usually considered modulo \(2\pi\), according to the notation adopted in the lesson.

## 12.2 Fundamental identities

\[
\sin^2 x+\cos^2 x=1
\]

\[
\cos(a+b)=\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b
\]

\[
\cos(a-b)=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b
\]

\[
\sin(a+b)=\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b
\]

\[
\sin(a-b)=\sin a\cos b-\cos a\sin b
\]

\[
\cos(2a)=\cos^2a-\sin^2a
\]

\[
\cos(2a)=2\cos^2a-1
\]

\[
\cos(2a)=1-2\sin^2a
\]

\[
\sin(2a)=2\sin a\cos a
\]

## 12.3 Basic trigonometric equations

### Equation \(\sin x=\sin\alpha\)

\[
x=\alpha+2k\pi
\]

or:

\[
x=\pi-\alpha+2k\pi,
\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}
\]

### Equation \(\cos x=\cos\alpha\)

\[
x=2k\pi+\alpha
\]

or:

\[
x=2k\pi-\alpha
\]

### Equation \(\tan x=\tan\alpha\)

\[
x=\alpha+k\pi,
\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}
\]

Restrict the solutions to the required interval, for example:

\[
[0,2\pi]\quad\text{or}\quad[0,\pi]
\]

## 12.4 Equation of the form

\[
a\cos x+b\sin x=c
\]

Set:

\[
R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}
\]

Choose \(\varphi\) such that:

\[
\cos\varphi=\frac aR,
\qquad
\sin\varphi=\frac bR
\]

Then:

\[
a\cos x+b\sin x=R\cos(x-\varphi)
\]

and solve the resulting trigonometric equation.

## 12.5 Trigonometric inequalities

1. Identify the trigonometric function.
2. Work on one convenient period.
3. Use the unit circle or a sign chart.
4. Write the solution in the required interval.
5. Add periodicity if the domain is \(\mathbb{R}\).

# 13. Point transformations and homothety

## 13.1 Central symmetry

If \(A'\) is the image of \(A\) under central symmetry with center \(O\), then \(O\) is the midpoint of \([AA']\):

\[
\overrightarrow{OA'}=-\overrightarrow{OA}
\]

## 13.2 Axial symmetry

It preserves:

- Distances.
- Angle measures.
- Collinearity.
- Parallelism.

## 13.3 Translation

If the translation vector is \(\vec u\), then:

\[
\overrightarrow{MM'}=\vec u
\]

It preserves lengths, angles, parallelism, and collinearity.

## 13.4 Rotation

A rotation preserves:

- Distances.
- Angles.
- Circles.
- Collinearity.

## 13.5 Homothety

If the center is \(O\) and the ratio is \(k\), then:

\[
\overrightarrow{OM'}=k\overrightarrow{OM}
\]

Important properties:

- If \(k>0\), the image is on the same side of the center.
- If \(k<0\), it is on the opposite side.
- Lengths are multiplied by \(|k|\).
- Areas are multiplied by \(k^2\).
- A line remains a line, or is invariant if it passes through the center.
- Parallelism and collinearity are preserved.

## 13.6 Homothety-exercise method

1. Identify the center and ratio.
2. Write the vector relation.
3. Use it to calculate coordinates or prove collinearity.
4. Use the preservation properties for parallelism, angles, and ratios.
5. For a locus, let the moving point be \(M(x,y)\) and convert the condition into an equation.

# 14. The dot product in the plane

## 14.1 Geometric definition

\[
\vec u\cdot\vec v=\|\vec u\|\|\vec v\|\cos(\vec u,\vec v)
\]

## 14.2 Coordinate definition

If:

\[
\vec u=(x,y),\qquad \vec v=(x',y')
\]

then:

\[
\vec u\cdot\vec v=xx'+yy'
\]

## 14.3 Important properties

\[
\vec u\cdot\vec v=\vec v\cdot\vec u
\]

\[
\vec u\cdot(\vec v+\vec w)=\vec u\cdot\vec v+\vec u\cdot\vec w
\]

\[
\vec u\cdot\vec u=\|\vec u\|^2
\]

\[
\|\vec u+\vec v\|^2=\|\vec u\|^2+\|\vec v\|^2+2\vec u\cdot\vec v
\]

\[
\|\vec u-\vec v\|^2=\|\vec u\|^2+\|\vec v\|^2-2\vec u\cdot\vec v
\]

## 14.4 Perpendicularity

\[
\vec u\perp\vec v
\Longleftrightarrow
\vec u\cdot\vec v=0
\]

## 14.5 Equation of a line using a normal vector

If a line passes through \(A(x_A,y_A)\) and has normal vector \(\vec n=(a,b)\), then:

\[
\vec n\cdot\overrightarrow{AM}=0
\]

so:

\[
a(x-x_A)+b(y-y_A)=0
\]

## 14.6 Equation of a circle

A circle with center \(I(a,b)\) and radius \(r\) has equation:

\[
(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2
\]

## 14.7 Proving perpendicularity

1. Calculate the two vectors.
2. Calculate their dot product.
3. If it is zero, state the theorem.
4. Write the conclusion.

# 15. Numerical sequences

## 15.1 Arithmetic sequence

If the difference is constant:

\[
u_{n+1}-u_n=r
\]

the sequence is arithmetic with common difference \(r\).

General term:

\[
u_n=u_0+nr
\]

or, starting from \(u_p\):

\[
u_n=u_p+(n-p)r
\]

## 15.2 Sum of an arithmetic sequence

\[
S=u_p+u_{p+1}+\cdots+u_n
\]

Number of terms:

\[
n-p+1
\]

Therefore:

\[
S=\frac{(n-p+1)(u_p+u_n)}2
\]

## 15.3 Geometric sequence

If:

\[
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=q
\]

or:

\[
u_{n+1}=qu_n
\]

then it is geometric with common ratio \(q\), provided the denominator is nonzero when the quotient is used.

General term:

\[
u_n=u_0q^n
\]

or:

\[
u_n=u_pq^{n-p}
\]

## 15.4 Sum of a geometric sequence

If \(q\ne1\):

\[
S=u_p+u_{p+1}+\cdots+u_n
=u_p\frac{1-q^{n-p+1}}{1-q}
\]

## 15.5 Studying monotonicity

Calculate:

\[
u_{n+1}-u_n
\]

- Positive: increasing.
- Negative: decreasing.
- Zero: constant.

If the terms are positive, one may sometimes study:

\[
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
\]

## 15.6 Sequence-exercise procedure

1. Write the first terms if requested.
2. Determine whether the sequence is arithmetic, geometric, or neither.
3. Find the difference or ratio.
4. Write the general term.
5. Calculate a sum if required.
6. Study monotonicity and the limit.
7. Interpret the result in the context of the problem.

# 16. Solid geometry

## 16.1 Coordinates of a point

In a space coordinate system:

\[
M(x,y,z)
\]

## 16.2 Vector between two points

If:

\[
A(x_A,y_A,z_A),
\quad B(x_B,y_B,z_B)
\]

then:

\[
\overrightarrow{AB}=(x_B-x_A,y_B-y_A,z_B-z_A)
\]

## 16.3 Distance between two points

\[
AB=\sqrt{(x_B-x_A)^2+(y_B-y_A)^2+(z_B-z_A)^2}
\]

## 16.4 Equation of a plane parallel to a coordinate plane

- Plane parallel to \((Oxy)\):

\[
z=c
\]

- Plane parallel to \((Oxz)\):

\[
y=c
\]

- Plane parallel to \((Oyz)\):

\[
x=c
\]

## 16.5 Parametric equation of a line in space

If the line passes through \(A(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) and has direction vector:

\[
\vec u=(a,b,c)
\]

its parametric representation is:

\[
\begin{cases}
x=x_0+at\\
y=y_0+bt\\
z=z_0+ct
\end{cases}
\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}
\]

## 16.6 Proving that a point belongs to a line

Substitute the coordinates of the point into the parametric representation and look for one value of \(t\) satisfying all three equations.

## 16.7 Proving coplanarity

Use linear relations between vectors. If one vector can be expressed as a linear combination of two independent vectors in the same plane, then the vectors belong to one plane.

## 16.8 Equation of a sphere

A sphere with center \(I(a,b,c)\) and radius \(r\) has equation:

\[
(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2+(z-c)^2=r^2
\]

## 16.9 Cylinder and cone of revolution

The equation depends on the axis of revolution and the center or vertex. Do not memorize one formula without identifying the data. Start from the definition of the distance to the axis or the vertex, then derive the equation.

## 16.10 Drawing a solid

1. Draw the axes using an isometric perspective.
2. Mark the known points.
3. Draw visible edges with solid lines.
4. Draw hidden edges with dashed lines when requested.
5. Label coordinates or important lengths.
6. Do not rely on the drawing alone to prove a property.

|---|---|

| Determine | Identification of the object with a justification |
| Prove / show | Data, theorem or property, reasoning, conclusion |
| Verify | Substitution or test establishing the statement |
| Deduce | Direct use of a previous result |
| Study | Organized stages appropriate to the topic |
| Discuss | Cases according to a parameter or condition |
| Represent graphically | Axes, scale, points, asymptotes, curve |
| Solve | Domain, transformations, solution set, checking |
| Interpret | Translate the mathematical result into the meaning of the problem |
| Calculate an expectation | Probability law, formula, substitution, interpretation |
| Prove collinearity | Proportional vectors, line equation, or barycenter |
| Prove perpendicularity | Zero dot product or a geometric property |

# 18. Fully written model answers

## Model 1: Studying the variation of a function

Let:

\[
f(x)=x^2-2x-3
\]

We have:

\[
D_f=\mathbb{R}
\]

Calculate the derivative:

\[
f'(x)=2x-2=2(x-1)
\]

Therefore:

\[
f'(x)<0\quad\text{when }x<1
\]

and:

\[
f'(x)>0\quad\text{when }x>1
\]

Hence the function is decreasing on:

\[
]-\infty,1]
\]

and increasing on:

\[
[1,+\infty[
\]

Also:

\[
f(1)=1-2-3=-4
\]

Therefore, the function has a minimum value equal to \(-4\) at \(x=1\).

## Model 2: Equation of a tangent

Let:

\[
f(x)=x^2
\]

at \(x_0=1\):

\[
f'(x)=2x,
\qquad f'(1)=2
\]

and:

\[
f(1)=1
\]

Thus the tangent equation is:

\[
y=2(x-1)+1
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{y=2x-1}
\]

## Model 3: Proving collinearity

Calculate:

\[
\overrightarrow{AB}=(2,4),
\qquad
\overrightarrow{AC}=(4,8)
\]

Since:

\[
\overrightarrow{AC}=2\overrightarrow{AB}
\]

the two vectors are parallel. Consequently:

\[
\boxed{A,B,C\text{ are collinear}}
\]

## Model 4: Proving perpendicularity

If:

\[
\vec u=(2,3),
\qquad
\vec v=(3,-2)
\]

then:

\[
\vec u\cdot\vec v=2\times3+3\times(-2)=6-6=0
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\vec u\perp\vec v}
\]

## Model 5: Probability

If the number of possible outcomes is \(20\), and the number of favorable outcomes is \(7\), then:

\[
P(A)=\frac{7}{20}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{P(A)=0.35}
\]

## Model 6: Arithmetic sequence

If:

\[
u_0=3,
\qquad r=2
\]

then:

\[
u_n=u_0+nr=3+2n
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{u_n=3+2n}
\]

Also:

\[
u_{n+1}-u_n=2>0
\]

so the sequence is increasing.

# 19. Examination strategy

## Before starting

- Read the entire paper.
- Begin with questions you know.
- Mark the question that requires more thought.
- Keep time for checking.

## While solving

- Number each question clearly.
- Do not mix exercises.
- If you find an error, cross out the incorrect part with one line and continue.
- Leave space between answers.
- Draw tables with a ruler.
- Make final results easy to identify.

## During checking

Check:

- Domain restrictions.
- Signs of derivatives.
- Directions in variation tables.
- Signs in inequalities.
- Denominator and square-root conditions.
- Trigonometric solution intervals.
- Sum of probabilities.
- Sequence indices.
- Units, when applicable.
- Whether every question was actually answered.

# 20. Common mistakes

1. Forgetting the domain of a function.
2. Cancelling a denominator factor without keeping the excluded value.
3. Thinking that \(f'(x)>0\) means \(f(x)>0\).
4. Writing a variation table without studying the derivative sign.
5. Forgetting to restrict trigonometric solutions to the requested interval.
6. Using the arithmetic-sequence formula for a geometric sequence.
7. Calculating a probability without defining the possible outcomes.
8. Proving collinearity from a drawing alone.
9. Using the dot product with incorrect vectors.
10. Confusing the center and ratio of a homothety.
11. Using a previous result without explaining how it applies.
12. Writing “therefore” without an argument before it.
13. Copying a video solution without understanding it.
14. Using a calculator instead of writing the proof.
15. Using a theorem that has not been taught without justification.

## Final summary

An excellent mathematics answer is not necessarily the longest one. It is the answer that combines:

1. Understanding the question.
2. Choosing the appropriate rule.
3. Checking the conditions of the rule.
4. Ordering the calculations.
5. Justifying every important transition.
6. Writing an explicit conclusion.
7. Checking the result against the meaning of the problem.

**Practical rule:**

> Given data → rule or theorem → application → calculation → result → conclusion.
